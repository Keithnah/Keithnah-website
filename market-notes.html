KL cuts flood hotspots to 14: how to check a home before you buy

Kuala Lumpur City Hall (DBKL) says the city's flash flood and waterlogging hotspots have fallen from more than 30 to 14 over the past three years. The work behind it includes drain upgrades, desilting of rivers and main drains, and better upkeep of detention ponds. Desilting at Sungai Toba has been stepped up to six times a year, with similar work planned for Sungai Kerayong. Federal Territories Minister Hannah Yeoh said in late October that the goal is to bring the number down to 10 by the end of 2026. DBKL has also set up a flash flood task force that goes out whenever rainfall passes 22mm an hour to clear blocked drain inlets. In early November, ahead of the rainy season, the city stepped up drainage checks. The rain is not waiting, though. On 2 November, flash floods hit several parts of the Klang Valley after an afternoon storm, including roads near the Federal Highway. Officials have said heavier rainfall is now pushing past what older drains were designed for, so bigger works such as underground storage are being fast-tracked.

Keith's take

I'm glad to see the hotspot list shrinking, but 14 is still 14, and a flood map is not something you want to learn about after you get your keys. Most KL condos are fine, yet a flooded basement car park or a cut-off access road can hurt both your daily life and your resale price. For home buyers, a little homework now saves a lot of stress later. For investors, flood history shows up in rental demand and valuations, so a cheap price near a known hotspot may not be a bargain. My simple checklist: visit the area right after heavy rain, not on a sunny Saturday. Ask the management office whether the basement or lifts have ever been flooded, and check the minutes of past meetings if you can. Look at where the entrance and car park sit compared with the nearest river or monsoon drain. Finally, make sure your home insurance covers flood. Malton's Ukay Spring brings gated landed homes to Ampang

Malton Bhd has launched the first phase of Ukay Spring, a 56-acre development in Ukay Perdana, Ampang. Phase 1 has 142 stratified landed homes in a gated-and-guarded community. The mix is three-storey semi-detached homes on 40ft by 90ft plots and 2.5-storey link villas on 22ft by 80ft plots. According to EdgeProp, the phase comes with a clubhouse, jogging paths and linear parks, and the developer is selling it as low-density hillside living. Ukay Perdana is an established, leafy neighbourhood on the north-east edge of Kuala Lumpur, known for bungalows and quiet streets. New landed launches this close to the city are rare, which is why projects like this tend to get attention. The bigger picture is that most new supply in the Klang Valley is high-rise, while demand for landed homes from families stays steady. Hillside sites need extra care, though: slope works, drainage and access roads all matter. Since Phase 1 is only the start of a 56-acre plan, later phases will shape traffic and the feel of the area over the next few years.

Keith's take

I like seeing genuine landed supply this close to KL City Centre, because good family homes with a bit of garden are getting harder to find. Ampang's Ukay area has a loyal following, and a well-run gated project can hold its value nicely over time. For home buyers, this suits families who want space and security but don't want to move far out to Semenyih or Rawang. For investors, landed homes usually rent more slowly than city condos, so treat this as a long-term hold rather than a quick rental play. Before you commit, check a few things. Ask for the land tenure and the expected strata title and completion timeline in writing. Look at the slope protection and drainage plans, and visit after heavy rain. Ask about monthly maintenance fees, since strata landed homes pay for guards and shared facilities. Drive the route to your office at peak hour. RTS Link opens in February 2027: which Johor Bahru areas gain most

The Johor Bahru-Singapore RTS Link is opening a little later than planned. Keith's take

My view: the RTS will help the parts of Johor Bahru that are a walk or a short ride from the station, not the whole city. For investors, rental demand around Bukit Chagar and the city centre is the most dependable part of the story, but new supply near the station means rents will not rise forever. Projects further out have to win tenants on their own merits. KL subsale average tops RM1.2m as luxury deals lift prices

The average price of a subsale home in Kuala Lumpur rose 52% from a year earlier to about RM1.2 million in the second quarter of 2026, according to Juwai IQI. The jump came mainly from deals of RM4 million and above, which rose by about half. In the first quarter, the city's average was about RM1.02 million. The typical KL buyer paid much less than that. Juwai IQI says the median subsale price in Kuala Lumpur rose 12.8% to RM530,000, and the national median rose 2.7% to RM380,000, its third quarterly rise in a row. Nearly seven in ten subsale purchases nationwide were priced at RM500,000 or below. The picture differs by state: Penang's median rose 17.1%, partly because a different mix of homes changed hands, while Selangor edged slightly lower. Juwai IQI expects more transactions in the second half of the year, with prices rising moderately and Johor doing best. Keep in mind this is an agency's sales data, not the official national index.

Keith's take

Do not let the RM1.2 million headline scare you, or excite you. An average jumps when a few very expensive homes sell, which is why the median, about RM530,000 in KL, is the number that describes what most people actually paid. For home buyers, this means good homes in Kuala Lumpur are still within reach for many budgets, but you need to compare prices building by building, not city by city. For investors, rising luxury deals show money is flowing into prime areas, but rent in those segments does not always rise as fast as prices, so check the yield before you buy. My checklist: look up recent transacted prices for the exact building and unit size, compare new launches nearby against subsale prices, ask what the realistic rent is today, and set your ceiling price before you view. Rehda: two in three would-be buyers fail to get a housing loan

More than two-thirds of would-be home buyers in Malaysia fail to get a housing loan, the Real Estate and Housing Developers' Association (Rehda) said earlier this year, based on a survey of 166 developers. Rehda's latest survey, of 181 members for the first half of 2026, again found that homes priced from RM500,001 to RM700,000 had the highest loan rejection rates, at 31% to 45% of deals. Loan rejection was the most common reason developers gave for unsold homes, ahead of high prices. Rehda president Datuk Ho Hon Sang said many applications were turned down because income was too low, credit card commitments were too high, or the buyer's financial documents were incomplete. Up to 59% of developers in the latest survey said they still held unsold completed homes at the end of June. These figures come from developers' experience, not from the banks, so treat them as a strong signal rather than an exact count. Still, the message is clear: getting the loan approved is now often harder than finding the right home.

Keith's take

I see this every month: buyers fall in love with a unit, pay the booking fee, and only then find out the bank will not lend enough. Get your loan position clear first, and the house hunt becomes much easier. For home buyers, the biggest fix is often small debts. A car loan, a personal loan or a credit card balance can count against you even if you pay on time, so clearing or reducing them before you apply can change the result. For investors buying a second home, banks look harder at your total commitments, and existing rent is not always counted in full, so leave a bigger safety margin. My checklist: ask two or three banks for a loan eligibility check before you book, pay down credit card balances, gather six months of payslips or, if you are self-employed, two years of tax returns and bank statements, and keep your instalment at a level you are comfortable with. Residential Tenancy Bill nears: what landlords and tenants should know

Malaysia is getting closer to its first dedicated law for renting a home. The Housing and Local Government Ministry says it is finalising a Residential Tenancy Bill to balance the rights and protections of tenants and landlords. Today there is no single law for most home rentals, so disputes over deposits, repairs or unpaid rent usually end up in the ordinary courts. Deputy Minister Datuk Aiman Athirah Sabu said the bill will clearly set out what landlords and tenants each must do, and make disputes quicker, easier and cheaper to settle. Earlier proposals described a dedicated tenancy tribunal that would hear claims such as rent, the terms of the agreement and the return of deposits. She has also said the government does not plan nationwide rent controls, so rents should still follow the local market. The minister said in February that the bill would be tabled this year, but it has not been tabled yet, and the final details are still unknown. Until it becomes law, your written tenancy agreement is still what protects you. More people are renting, and lawmakers have urged the government to move faster.

Keith's take

I welcome this law, and I think good landlords have little to fear from it. Clear rules and a cheaper way to settle disputes usually make renting out a home less risky, not more, because the rules apply to both sides. For investors, the practical step is to run every tenancy as if the new rules were already here: a proper written agreement, a clear deposit amount, and photos of the unit on move-in and move-out day. For home buyers who plan to rent out later, check the building's house rules and the likely rent before you buy, because your instalment should not depend on a perfect tenant. My checklist for landlords: use a stamped written tenancy agreement, list what the deposit covers and when it will be returned, keep receipts for repairs and payments, and agree in writing who fixes what. For tenants: read the agreement, ask for a move-in inventory and keep proof of every payment. WhatsApp me if you want help working out the rent for a unit.

Ringgit firms after OPR hold: what it means for foreign buyers

The ringgit strengthened after Bank Negara Malaysia kept the Overnight Policy Rate at 2.75% on 5 November, at its last policy meeting of the year. By the close that day it stood at about 4.04 to the US dollar, firmer than about 4.06 the day before. The rate has now stayed at 2.75% since July 2025. Bank Negara said growth stayed strong in the third quarter, helped by exports and spending at home, and expects the economy to grow by more than 5% in 2026. The governor said the current rate fits the outlook for prices and growth, so there is no need to tighten for now. Some economists still think a small rise in 2027 cannot be ruled out. For buyers who earn in another currency, a stronger ringgit makes the same home cost a little more. Exchange rates move every day, so the size of the change can grow or shrink quickly. Loan rates in Malaysia are likely to stay where they are for now, but a rise next year is possible.

Keith's take

I would not rush or delay a purchase because of one week of currency moves. As a simple example, at 4.06 a RM1 million home costs about US$246,200; at 4.04 it is about US$247,200, roughly US$1,000 more. That matters, but the right unit at the right price matters far more. For foreign and MM2H buyers, the bigger question is how you will pay. If you are funding the deposit and progress payments from abroad, you are exposed to the exchange rate at every stage, not just on the day you book. For investors, remember that rent comes in ringgit, so a firmer ringgit also lifts the value of that income in your home currency. My checklist: work out your budget in ringgit first, ask your bank about converting in stages rather than all at once, keep a buffer of about 5% for currency swings, and if you plan to borrow here, check how your instalment would change if rates rise by 0.25%. Longer home loans, higher prices? What experts say about tenures

Housing experts are warning that bigger loans and longer repayment periods are part of why homes in Malaysia feel so hard to afford. Longer loans lower the monthly instalment, so a home looks cheaper, but the buyer pays much more interest over the life of the loan. Housing loans already made up about 60% of all household debt in 2023. Dr Suraya Ismail of the Khazanah Research Institute says stretched tenures and ideas such as intergenerational loans only make homes appear more affordable. KRI has advised against loans of up to 60 years and wants more homes priced for middle-income households instead. Joe Thor of Infomina Geolytik says only about 10% of new homes are affordable to households earning below RM6,000 a month, while economist Yeah Kim Leng sees financing as a supporting factor rather than the main driver of prices. Bank Negara has long held that a maximum tenure of 35 years is enough for most borrowers to repay before retirement. The real fix, most experts agree, is building enough homes at prices that match local incomes, which takes years.

Keith's take

I agree with the experts here: a longer loan is a tool, not a discount. Bank Negara's own example years ago showed a RM300,000 loan at 4.65% costing about RM70 less a month over 40 years instead of 35, but about RM53,000 more in total. The home did not get cheaper; the bill just got longer. For home buyers, choose the tenure that keeps your instalment comfortable today, then pay extra when you can, because most housing loans let you reduce the balance early. For investors, a longer tenure can help cash flow while a unit is rented, but only if the rent still covers the instalment with room to spare. My checklist: keep the instalment under about a third of your take-home pay, compare the total interest for 30 and 35 years, ask your bank about early repayment and any lock-in period, and look at homes under RM500,000 if the numbers feel tight. First-home stamp duty waiver runs to 2027: how buyers under RM500k save

Malaysians buying their first home priced at RM500,000 or less pay no stamp duty on the transfer document or the loan agreement until 31 December 2027. The extension was announced in Budget 2026. For a RM500,000 home, Savills estimates the saving at about RM9,000 on the transfer plus roughly RM2,000 on the loan agreement. The same Budget doubled the Housing Credit Guarantee Scheme (SJKP) to RM20 billion. The scheme guarantees home loans for buyers who find it hard to get financing, including gig workers and the self-employed who have no fixed monthly salary. It aims to help up to about 80,000 first-time buyers, and by September 2025 it had already backed about RM23 billion in loans for over 97,000 people. The waiver is only for Malaysian citizens buying their first home, and the RM500,000 cap is strict. These measures come as banks approve fewer housing loans, so preparing your loan early matters more than ever.

Keith's take

About RM11,000 saved is real money for a first home, roughly the cost of furnishing a small unit or a few months of instalments. If you are a first-time buyer and your budget is around RM500,000, it is worth staying under the cap, because going even slightly above it means you lose the full exemption. Timing also matters. The waiver ends on 31 December 2027, so for a new launch, ask your lawyer when the transfer and loan documents will be stamped, and make sure that falls before the deadline. If you are self-employed or work freelance, ask your bank about SJKP early, because a guarantee can be the difference between a rejection and an approval. My checklist for first-time buyers: confirm you have never owned a home, keep the price at or below RM500,000, get your loan pre-approval or SJKP eligibility checked first, and then shortlist. 8% stamp duty for foreign buyers: how MM2H buyers should budget

Since 1 January 2026, foreigners who buy a home in Malaysia pay a flat 8% stamp duty on the property transfer, double the earlier 4%. The rate was announced in Budget 2026 and applies to non-citizens and foreign companies buying residential property, both new launches and resale units. Permanent residents, and commercial or industrial property, are not covered by this change. The change matters for MM2H participants because no special exemption was announced for them. In February, Tourism Minister Tiong King Sing said 744 MM2H participants had bought homes in Malaysia up to the end of 2025, led by buyers from China (304), Taiwan (91) and Singapore (63). He also said thousands more participants were still at the buying stage. MM2H status also does not replace state rules: foreign buyers still need state approval and must meet each state's minimum purchase price. Industry consultants say the higher duty has made foreign buyers more selective rather than driving them away.

Keith's take

For a foreign buyer, the 8% is a real cost that is paid upfront and usually in cash, on top of the deposit and legal fees. On a RM1.5 million home, stamp duty on the transfer alone is about RM120,000, so it has to be in your budget from day one rather than discovered at the signing table. I suggest MM2H buyers plan in this order: first confirm your MM2H tier and its minimum property value, then check the state's minimum price for foreigners and whether the unit has foreign-buyer approval, and only then shortlist projects. Ask for a full cost sheet before you book, covering the price, 8% stamp duty, legal fees, loan costs if any, and the first year of maintenance. Because the extra duty makes every purchase more expensive to enter and exit, it also rewards buying for the long term. Choose a home you would be happy to keep for many years, in a location with lasting demand. New short-stay rental rules: check before you buy to rent out

The Housing and Local Government Ministry is working to standardise the rules for short-term rental accommodation across Malaysia. Under the proposed framework, only premises that are registered and fully meet their local council's licensing conditions would be allowed to operate short-stay rentals. In early October the planning guidelines were in a final review with the Tourism Ministry before going to the Cabinet, so they are not yet final. The proposed process would have hosts first obtain a licence from the local council and then register the unit as tourist accommodation. Planned changes to the Tourism Industry Act would also cover insurance, platform registration and stronger enforcement. Some states are already moving on their own. Penang's short-stay accommodation by-laws took effect on 1 August 2026 and require operators to get a licence from their city council, and on Penang Island short-stays are not allowed in stratified residential buildings. Other states are expected to follow once the national guidelines are approved.

Keith's take

If you are buying a unit mainly to earn short-stay income, the rules are changing under your feet, so the safest plan is one that still works as a normal long-term rental. Run your numbers on monthly rent first, and treat any short-stay income as a bonus rather than the reason the deal works. Before you book, ask three questions. Does the building's deed of mutual covenant or house rules allow short stays? Is the land title and zoning commercial or residential, since that affects licensing? And what has the local council in that area said about licences? If a sales pitch promises high short-stay returns, ask for the answers in writing. In Penang this is already decided for many buildings, and other states will set their own conditions soon. For investors, well-located homes with steady long-term tenant demand, near rail lines, hospitals, universities or business districts, remain the more reliable choice. Keith's take

New industrial parks bring jobs, and jobs bring demand for homes to rent and buy nearby. That is the long-term case for southern Johor, but timing matters: this park will be built in stages up to 2030, so the effect on housing will come gradually, not overnight. For investors, I would look for homes that already make sense today, with good road access to Iskandar Puteri and Johor Bahru, reasonable maintenance fees and a price that does not assume the park is already full. Johor still has a large supply of unsold homes, so choose carefully and do not pay a premium just because a new park was announced. My checklist: check the travel time from the project to the industrial areas, compare rents for similar units nearby, and make sure the numbers work even if prices stay flat for a few years. Bank Negara holds the OPR at 2.75%: what it means for your home loan

Bank Negara Malaysia kept the Overnight Policy Rate (OPR) at 2.75% at its final Monetary Policy Committee meeting of 2026 on 5 November. It is the eighth meeting in a row without a change. The central bank said growth stayed strong in the third quarter, helped by exports and steady spending at home, and it now expects the economy to grow by more than 5% this year. Governor Abdul Rasheed Ghaffour said there is no immediate need to raise rates while inflation stays contained, but the bank is watching whether wage increases start to push prices up. The ringgit firmed after the decision, and the next meeting is on 21 January 2027. In its statement, the central bank listed the main risks to the outlook: prolonged geopolitical tensions, tighter global financial conditions, concerns over market valuations and fiscal weaknesses abroad. After the announcement the ringgit closed at about 4.045 to the US dollar, up from about 4.061 the day before. Before the meeting, some banks had flagged the chance of a 0.25% rise to 3.00% in the first half of 2027, so the next few decisions will still be watched closely.

JLL Q3 briefing: Kuala Lumpur property is recovering, slowly

Property consultancy JLL presented its third-quarter 2026 findings on 10 November and described the market as recovering gradually. The clearest trend is a move to quality: companies are relocating to newer, better-built offices, and that demand is lifting occupancy in the best buildings in Greater Kuala Lumpur. On the residential side, the number and value of home sales improved modestly in the second quarter after a sharp dip at the start of the year, but they were still slightly lower than a year earlier. JLL has said it expects homes prices to stay broadly stable this year, with affordability the biggest thing holding buyers back. Looking ahead, JLL Malaysia managing director Jamie Tan said tighter loan approvals and possibly higher borrowing costs could be the main challenge for the housing market in 2027. He linked a possible rise in the Overnight Policy Rate to banks becoming stricter with lending. This follows EdgeProp's report a few days earlier that the housing loan approval ratio had fallen below 40% for the first time since 2021.

Keith's take

A slow recovery is good news for careful buyers. Prices are not running away, so you have time to compare projects, ask developers for their best terms and negotiate, instead of rushing in. The flight to quality in offices matters for homes too: the people who work in those better buildings want to rent nearby. Projects close to MRT and LRT stations, with good build quality and sensible maintenance fees, are the ones most likely to stay in demand. If you are buying to rent out, put location and quality ahead of the lowest price. The loan side is the part I would watch most. With approvals getting harder, a buyer who prepares early has a real advantage: check your CCRIS record, keep your debt service ratio comfortable, and have your income documents ready before you book a unit. For projects, ask the developer which panel banks they work with and what the end-financing timeline looks like, because a smooth loan process matters as much as the price.
