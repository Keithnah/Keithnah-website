A Beginner's Guide to Investing in Malaysian Property as a Foreigner

2 Jul 2026 · Keith Nah

Foreigners can legally buy most condominiums in Malaysia, subject to a minimum purchase price that varies by state — in Kuala Lumpur, that threshold currently sits at RM1 million for most property types. There's no need for permanent residency or a work visa to buy; a valid passport and the funds to meet the threshold are the starting point for a first purchase.

What You Can — And Can't — Buy

Foreign buyers are generally free to purchase condominiums, serviced apartments and commercial units above the state minimum price, but landed residential property (terrace houses, bungalows, land) is more tightly restricted and, in many states, requires additional state authority consent or is simply off-limits to foreign buyers entirely. This is one of the most common points of confusion for first-time buyers browsing listings without a local guide.

Some developments are also built specifically as "Malaysia My Second Home"-friendly or foreign-quota units within a project, with a portion of units reserved for local buyers under Bumiputera or general local-buyer quotas. Understanding a project's foreign-buyer quota before falling in love with a specific unit will save a lot of disappointment later in the process.

The Purchase Process, Step By Step

The process itself follows a familiar structure: sign a booking form and pay a deposit (typically around 2–3% of the price), then move to a formal Sale and Purchase Agreement within a set number of weeks, followed by loan application if financing, and finally the Memorandum of Transfer once the purchase completes and any required state consent has been granted.

Most transactions take three to six months from booking to completion for secondary market units, and considerably longer for new developer launches, which are governed by a staged progress-payment schedule tied to construction milestones rather than a single lump-sum settlement.

Where Most Delays Actually Happen

Where foreign buyers most often need guidance is in coordinating the legal, banking and state consent steps in the right order — getting this sequence wrong is the most common cause of delays, particularly for states that require formal state authority consent before a foreign purchase can be registered, which can add weeks or months if not initiated early.

Working with an agent and a lawyer experienced specifically in foreign purchases is the easiest way to avoid that. A generalist conveyancing lawyer who rarely handles foreign transactions may not flag a state consent requirement until it's already causing a delay — by which point renegotiating timelines with a developer or seller becomes much harder.
